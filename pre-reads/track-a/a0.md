# Unit A0 - What an FDE Actually Is

## Start with a scenario

A business stakeholder messages you: *"Renewals are taking too long. Can
you look into it?"*

A typical engineer's first instinct: ask for a ticket, wait for acceptance
criteria, maybe ask "too long compared to what?" and wait for an answer.
Weeks pass. Nothing ships, because nobody - including the business - knows
yet what "fixed" looks like.

A Forward Deployed Engineer's first move is different: go find out what
"renewals" actually involves, today, as it's really done - not as anyone
describes it in a meeting. Then produce *something* - a rough diagram of
the steps, a 10-line script that counts how many renewals sat untouched for
more than 5 days, anything concrete - and put it in front of the
stakeholder. Their reaction to that small, imperfect thing is worth more
than another round of questions.

That reflex - **go find out, then show something small, fast** - is the
core of the role. Everything else in this track is detail on how to do that
well, especially when you personally don't know the domain or the tech
stack well enough to do it alone (more on that in Unit A2).

## The principle

**An FDE owns the outcome, not a stage of a process.** Most engineering
roles are a stage in a relay: requirements arrive, you build, you hand off
to test, someone else deploys. Each stage is accountable for its own
artifact - the ticket, the code, the test pass - but nobody is accountable
for whether the business problem actually got solved.

An FDE refuses that framing. If renewals are still slow after the "fix"
shipped, that is the FDE's problem, not a gap between stages. This means an
FDE is involved from "business isn't sure what they want" all the way to
"did the number actually move" - not just the build-and-ship middle.

## What an FDE is NOT

- **Not a lone genius who knows the whole domain and stack solo.** In a
  deep, legacy-heavy environment, nobody does. The skill is knowing how to
  pull in the right help fast (Unit A2), not knowing everything yourself.
- **Not someone who waits for a clear spec.** If you're waiting for clarity
  that doesn't exist yet, you've already failed the role - manufacturing
  that clarity is the job (Unit A1).
- **Not someone who reaches for the first familiar solution.** Jumping to
  "I know how to build X" before confirming X is the smallest useful thing
  is exactly the narrow-focus trap this track exists to correct (Unit A3).
