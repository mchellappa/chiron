# Unit A1 - Why Business Can't Hand You a Clear Ask

## Start with two asks that sound the same but aren't

**Ask 1:** "Make renewals faster." The stakeholder has never measured
renewal time, doesn't know where the delay happens, and honestly isn't sure
what "faster" would even look like in a dashboard. They just feel the pain.

**Ask 2:** "Make renewals faster." Same words - but this stakeholder has
already seen the data, knows renewals stall at the underwriting handoff
specifically, and has a number in mind (under 48 hours), but never wrote
any of that down because they assumed it was obvious.

These require completely different responses. Treat them the same and you
will either overbuild discovery for Ask 2 (wasting time re-deriving what
they already knew) or underbuild it for Ask 1 (building the wrong thing
fast, confidently).

## The principle

**Ambiguity has (at least) two different causes, and they need different
cures:**

1. **Vision doesn't exist yet.** Nobody - including the business - has
   actually decided what "better" means. There is no hidden answer to
   extract; there is a decision that hasn't been made. Your job is
   **co-discovery**: help them figure out what they actually want, usually
   by showing them something concrete and watching what they react to.
2. **Vision exists but wasn't communicated.** The business already knows
   what they want; it's in someone's head, or in old notes, or implicit in
   how they've always worked. Your job is **extraction**: better questions,
   talking to the right person, reading what's already there - not
   building prototypes to "discover" something that's already known.

Confusing these two is the single most common way clarity work goes wrong.
If you run a discovery/prototyping exercise on an Ask-2 situation, you waste
cycles re-learning what one conversation would have told you. If you ask
"what exactly do you want" repeatedly on an Ask-1 situation, you get
silence or a shrug, because the honest answer is "I don't know yet" and
they're too embarrassed to say it.

## How to tell them apart, fast

Ask one diagnostic question early: **"If I built the obvious first guess
right now, what would you check first?"**

- If they immediately describe a specific metric, screen, or scenario,
  that's evidence of Ask 2 - vision exists, go extract it precisely.
- If they hesitate, give a vague answer, or say "just show me something and
  I'll know it when I see it," that's Ask 1 - go build the smallest
  concrete thing and use their reaction as the real requirement.

## Worked example

Business says: "The onboarding experience for new advisors feels clunky."

You ask the diagnostic question: "If I fixed the obvious thing right now -
say, cut the number of screens in half - what's the first thing you'd
check?" They say: "Honestly... I'd just want to see if it *feels* faster. I
don't have a specific number in mind."

That's Ask 1. Vision doesn't exist. Correct move: build a rough
click-through mockup of a shorter flow (not real code, not integrated -
just enough to react to), show it this week, and let their reaction define
what "clunky" actually meant.
