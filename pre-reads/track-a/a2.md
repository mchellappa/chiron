# Unit A2 - Manufacturing Clarity Through Orchestration

## Start with the trap

You diagnosed an Ask-1 situation (Unit A1): vision doesn't exist yet, you
need to show something concrete to get a real reaction. Natural next
thought: "Great, I'll build a quick prototype myself."

Here's the trap: in a deep domain (retirement rules, actuarial logic,
regulatory nuance) running on a legacy stack, "quick" and "myself" rarely
go together. If you try to be the lone person who understands the domain
well enough AND can move fast in the stack, you will either take too long
learning the domain (and the window for a fast reaction closes), or you'll
build something domain-wrong fast (and the stakeholder's reaction tells you
nothing useful, because they're reacting to an obviously broken premise,
not the real question).

Neither outcome manufactures clarity. Both waste the moment.

## The principle

**The smallest unit of progress is rarely "code I wrote alone." It's often
"the right person or tool brought in for 15 minutes."** An FDE's actual
skill in this environment is not knowing everything - it's rapidly
identifying the one gap that's blocking progress and closing it through
orchestration:

- **A domain expert**, for a short, targeted conversation - not a full
  knowledge transfer, just enough to not be obviously wrong
- **An AI agent**, for a first-pass artifact (a draft data model, a rough
  script, a summary of how an existing system behaves) that you then
  validate rather than build from scratch
- **An existing system or document**, that already encodes the answer, if
  you know to go look rather than reinvent

The discipline is noticing, fast, "I am the bottleneck right now" - and
routing around yourself instead of grinding through a knowledge gap alone.

## How to tell when you're the bottleneck

Ask yourself, honestly, before you start building: **"Am I about to spend
more than an hour becoming competent enough in something I'll only need
once?"**

If yes - that's a signal to orchestrate (find the expert, use the agent,
find the doc) rather than grind. If no - if it's genuinely fast for you to
just do it - then do it yourself; orchestration has its own overhead and
isn't free.

## Worked example

Continuing the advisor-onboarding scenario from Unit A1: you need a rough
click-through mockup of a shorter flow. You don't know the current flow's
exact screens well enough to shorten it credibly, and the system that
renders it is a legacy portal you've never touched.

Trap move: spend two days reverse-engineering the legacy portal's screens
yourself so your mockup is accurate.

Orchestrated move:
1. Message whoever owns onboarding support tickets: "Can you screenshot the
   current 8 screens for me? 10 minutes." (closes the domain gap)
2. Ask an agent to draft a simplified flow (4 screens) based on those
   screenshots and a one-line goal ("cut repeated data entry").
   (closes the "build it fast" gap)
3. You review the agent's draft for anything domain-obviously-wrong before
   showing it - you're validating, not authoring from scratch.
4. Show the result to the stakeholder within the day, not the week.

You never became an expert in the legacy portal. You didn't need to. You
needed the artifact to exist fast enough to get a real reaction.

## The failure mode this corrects

This is the direct antidote to narrow-focus/tunnel-vision (the first
failure mode identified for this program): an engineer who defaults to "let
me go learn this properly first" is optimizing for their own competence,
not for the speed of manufacturing clarity. The orchestration reflex keeps
the focus on the outcome (a reaction from the stakeholder, fast) instead of
the engineer's personal mastery of the domain.
